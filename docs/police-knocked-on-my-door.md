# Why Did the Police Knock on My Door?

**Most door-knocks are about your street, not about you — canvassing after a
nearby incident, a welfare check someone requested, or a previous resident's
paper trail — and you can find out which without opening the door: ask
through it, or call the non-emergency line and ask whether an officer was
sent to your address.** Here's the reason list, the verification habit worth
having, and what actually happens with each type.

<div class="tldr" markdown="1">
<span class="tldr__label">Key takeaways</span>
**Usually:** canvassing ("did your cameras catch anything?"), a welfare
check, a wrong/old address, found property, a complaint. **Verify:** call
**non-emergency dispatch** — "did you send an officer to my address?" Real
officers wait; impostors pressure. **Your call:** you don't have to open
without a warrant; through-the-door works. **Card on the door:** call back
when ready — it's rarely urgent. **What was the incident they're canvassing
about?** That's a [police-activity lookup](/police-activity-near-me/).

## The reason list, decoded

- **Canvassing** — something happened nearby ([the thing with the
  sirens](/sirens-near-me/), a burglary, a hit-and-run) and officers are
  walking the block for witnesses and **doorbell-camera footage** — the
  modern reason for most knocks. Sharing a clip is voluntary and often
  genuinely decisive.
- **Welfare check** — someone asked police to confirm a person at your
  address is okay. Seeing that person alive ends the call; stonewalling
  extends it, because "confirm they're okay" is the entire mission.
- **Wrong or stale address** — a previous tenant's records, a mistyped
  house number on a call. State it plainly; recurring visits get fixed
  through the records division.
- **Found property / notifications** — your wallet turned in, your car
  window smashed down the street, a death notification (officers in pairs,
  asking to come in).
- **Complaints** — noise, parking, the dog. A conversation, occasionally a
  citation.
- **Alarm response** — your alarm company called them; they'll walk the
  perimeter and want to know it's false.

## The verification habit (use it every time)

Uniform-shaped pressure at a door is also a burglary and impersonation
tactic, and real officers know it. So: **talk through the door or camera,
ask what it's about, and if anything feels off, call the non-emergency
line and ask "did you send an officer to my address?"** Dispatch confirms
in seconds; a genuine officer waits politely, and a fake one leaves — the
call itself is the test. Chain or camera beats cracked door while you do
it. (911 if the person becomes aggressive or tries the handle.)

## What you do and don't have to do

You don't have to open the door, answer questions, or hand over camera
footage without a warrant — and declining, politely, ends most encounters
("we'll leave a card"). If a **warrant** is announced, ask to see it
through the window: an *arrest* warrant for a person, or a *search*
warrant for your address, changes the situation entirely — comply and
sort disputes out later through counsel, never at the threshold. The
practical middle path most people actually want: find out what happened
on the street (ask; officers canvassing will usually tell you the
outline), help if you can, and skip anything you're uneasy about.
