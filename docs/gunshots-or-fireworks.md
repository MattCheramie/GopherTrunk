# Gunshots or Fireworks? How to Tell — and How to Find Out

**By ear alone, even experienced listeners routinely get this wrong — echo,
distance, and buildings smear the differences. The reliable answer isn't in the
sound; it's in what happens next: whether a "shots heard" call goes out, and
what the responding officers actually find.** Here's the honest acoustic
guidance, and then the part nobody tells you — how to hear the outcome.

<div class="tldr" markdown="1">
<span class="tldr__label">Key takeaways</span>
**Lean gunfire:** uniform sharp cracks, irregular deliberate rhythm, no
whistle/crackle, nothing visible. **Lean fireworks:** varied bangs, whistles,
crackles, clusters, flashes above rooflines. **Unsure and concerned? Call
911** — that's a legitimate call. **The real answer:** the "shots heard"
dispatch and the officers' findings, live on a
[scanner feed](/listen-to-police-scanner-online/) or
[your own scanner](#kit) within minutes.
</div>

## The by-ear checklist (and its limits)

Lean **gunfire** when the bangs are sharp, uniform cracks — the same sound
repeated — in an irregular or deliberate rhythm, with no whistle, no crackle,
and nothing lighting up the sky. Lean **fireworks** when reports vary
shot-to-shot, whistle on the way up or crackle after, arrive in celebratory
clusters, or pair with flashes above the rooflines.

Now the honest part: urban echo makes single distant fireworks sound sharp,
and gunfire around a holiday hides inside the fireworks baseline. Cities
deploy acoustic sensor networks (ShotSpotter) precisely because human ears —
including trained ones — misclassify these sounds constantly. Treat the
checklist as a lean, not a verdict.

## What to do in the moment

If you suspect gunfire: stay inside, away from windows, and **call 911** with
what you heard, how many, and the direction. "Shots heard" is one of the most
common calls dispatchers take; a fireworks false alarm costs nothing, a missed
shooting costs a lot. Don't go outside to investigate, and don't post to
Nextdoor *instead of* calling.

## Finding out what it actually was

This is where the radio settles it. Within a few minutes of any 911 calls:

- **The dispatch goes out** — "shots heard, [your block]" — on the police
  channel, audible on a [county scanner feed](/listen-to-police-scanner-online/)
  or your own receiver.
- **The outcome follows** — officers report "fireworks confirmed," "unable to
  locate," or evidence found. That closing traffic is the answer no app gives
  you.
- **The CAD log** shows the call type and disposition slightly later; the
  methods in [police activity near me](/police-activity-near-me/) and
  [what happened last night](/what-happened-near-me-last-night/) apply here
  too.
- **Silence on the radio** usually means nobody reported it at all — which is
  itself useful to know when you were the only one awake at 1 a.m.

One caveat: in cities that have [encrypted police
dispatch](/police-scanner-encryption/), the radio route is closed — check
[RadioReference](https://www.radioreference.com/) for your agency, and lean on
the CAD log instead.

## If it was close: two checklists

**What 911 actually needs from you** (worth rehearsing once):

- How many bangs, over how many seconds, and the spacing (even/irregular).
- Direction and rough distance — "northeast, a block or two" beats "outside."
- Anything paired with it: vehicles accelerating, voices, breaking glass.
- Whether you *saw* anything — muzzle flash low to the ground vs fireworks
  overhead settles it instantly for the call-taker.

**If you think your home or car may have been struck:** don't touch or dig
anything out — an impact mark and the round behind it are evidence. Photograph
it, note the time of the bangs, and report it even a day later; a documented
strike changes how the original call is classified and patrolled. And if a
window or wall was hit while you were home, report immediately, not in the
morning.
