**A marine scanner is a fantastic listening tool and a dangerous thing to mistake

licensed VHF radio that actually keeps you safe on the water. Here's the honest
setup for boaters.

## Safety first: a scanner is not a marine radio

Say it plainly: a scanner is **receive-only**. It cannot key up channel 16 to call
for help, and it can't send a [DSC](/reference/dsc/) distress alert with your
position. Every boater still needs a **proper marine VHF radio** — a fixed-mount or
handheld unit, properly licensed, ideally DSC-equipped and tied to your MMSI. A
marine scanner is a **listening supplement** for monitoring extra traffic; treat it
as an add-on, never a replacement for the radio that calls the Coast Guard.

## The marine VHF band

Marine voice lives in **156–162 MHz** and is **analog FM**, so a cheap analog
scanner receives it clearly. The channels a boater cares about:

- **Channel 16 — 156.800 MHz.** International distress, safety, and calling. Always
  monitored.
- **Channel 09 — 156.450 MHz.** Common secondary hailing / recreational calling.
- **Working, bridge, lock, and port-operations channels** vary by waterway.
- **NOAA marine weather — 162.400–162.550 MHz**, just above the marine band. See the
  [frequency guide](/scanner-frequencies/).
- **Channel 70 — DSC.** A digital signaling channel for distress/calling data, not
  routine voice — and something only a real [DSC](/reference/dsc/) radio uses.

## Why analog, and why not digital

Marine VHF voice is analog, so there is no reason to buy a
[digital P25](/reference/project-25/) scanner for boating — the
[BC125AT](/reference/uniden-bc125at/) hears everything on the water. The only
digital element, [DSC](/reference/dsc/) on channel 70, is signaling data your real
marine radio handles, not something you monitor as voice.
