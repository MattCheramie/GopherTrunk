# Why Are There Fire Trucks on My Street (But No Fire)?

**In most of the US, the majority of fire-engine responses aren't fires — they
are medical calls, automatic alarms, gas odors, and assists, which is why the
trucks show up with no smoke anywhere in sight.** The number of vehicles tells
you what was *reported*, not what was found. Here's how to read the scene, and
how to get the actual answer in about a minute.

## The no-fire calls that send fire trucks

- **Medical calls** — the biggest share of fire department call volume by
  far. Engines are dispatched with (often ahead of) the ambulance because
  stations are distributed for response time and crews are EMT-trained.
- **Automatic fire alarms** — a huge fraction are false (cooking, dust,
  dying batteries), but every activation gets a response.
- **Gas odors and carbon-monoxide alarms** — crews investigate with meters;
  most readings are clean.
- **Lift assists and welfare checks** — helping a fallen resident up, or
  forcing entry when someone inside may be in trouble.
- **Crashes, wires down, water problems, animal rescues** — the "anything
  hazardous that isn't police work" bucket.

## Why five trucks for one house

Dispatch protocols attach a **pre-planned response package** to each call
type. A *reported* structure fire in a typical suburb automatically sends
multiple engines, a ladder, a battalion chief, and an ambulance — before
anyone knows whether it's a working fire or a smoking pot roast. First crew
on scene sizes it up and cancels the rest, which is why you often see trucks
turn around mid-street. Lots of apparatus = serious *report*; trucks leaving
quickly = it wasn't.
