# Accident Near Me? How to Find Out What Happened

**A crash is dispatched over the radio within seconds of the first 911 call —
location, lanes blocked, injuries, extrication — long before any map turns red
or any article posts.** Whether you heard the bang from your kitchen or you're
staring at brake lights on the highway, here's how to get the real answer, in
the order the sources actually update.

## Right now: the radio has it first

Every crash response — police, fire, EMS — is coordinated on dispatch
channels that are [legal to hear](/police-scanner-legal/) and usually
unencrypted (fire/EMS especially). Within the first minute you'll hear:

- **Location** — street and cross-street or highway milepost.
- **Severity** — "property damage only," "injury accident," or "extrication"
  (someone trapped; the serious ones).
- **Lane status** — what's blocked and for roughly how long.

A [Broadcastify feed](/listen-to-police-scanner-online/) for your county
carries this if a volunteer runs one; your own
[scanner](/how-police-scanners-work/) carries it always.

## Minutes later: the traffic layer

- **State 511 / DOT map** — every state runs one (search `"<state> 511"`).
  Confirmed highway incidents, lane closures, and clearance estimates;
  surface streets mostly absent.
- **Waze / Google Maps** — crowd-reported crash pins and the red slowdown
  trail. Fast on busy roads, blind on quiet ones, and never says *what*
  happened.
- **Agency active-calls page** — the CAD entry ("MVA," "10-50," "TC") with a
  block address; search `"<county> active calls"`.

## Reading severity from your window

The response tells the story before any report does: one police cruiser is a
fender-bender; an engine and ambulance mean reported injuries; a second
ambulance, rescue company, or a
[helicopter overhead](/helicopter-circling-my-neighborhood/) mean a major
incident; and a slow trickle of arriving units usually means it sounded worse
on the 911 call than it was.
