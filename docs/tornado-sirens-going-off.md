# Why Are the Tornado Sirens Going Off Right Now?

**If outdoor warning sirens are sounding and it's not a scheduled test time,
assume a warning covers your area and get information immediately — sirens are
activated county-wide or by zone, so clear sky at your house proves nothing
about a storm twenty miles away.** Here's what activation actually means, how
to confirm the reason in seconds, and why the siren must never be your only
warning system.

## What siren activation actually means

Outdoor warning sirens (the pole-mounted "civil defense" sirens) have exactly
one job: tell people **who are outdoors** to get inside and seek information.
They are not designed to wake you, name the hazard, or tell you when it ends.

Activation policy is **local** — set by county or city emergency management —
which is why the same siren means slightly different things in different
places. Typical policies:

- **Tornado warning** issued for the county or the siren's zone (the near
  universal trigger).
- **Severe thunderstorm warning** with destructive winds (often 70+ mph) or
  very large hail, in many jurisdictions.
- **Other emergencies** — hazardous-materials releases, dam failure — in some
  communities.
- **Scheduled tests**, usually a fixed monthly or weekly time.

## Confirm the reason in under 30 seconds

1. **Phone:** any weather app's warning map, or the Wireless Emergency Alert
   that may have arrived with it.
2. **[NOAA weather radio](/best-emergency-radios/)** (162.400–162.550 MHz):
   the broadcast will be mid-warning if one is active. A receiver with SAME
   alerting turns itself on for your county specifically.
3. **County emergency management** on Facebook/X — activations and tests are
   usually posted within minutes.
4. **Local broadcast media**, which simulcast warnings immediately.

Near noon, calm skies, first Wednesday or Saturday of the month? Probably your
community's test — but verify the schedule on your county's site once, today,
so you never have to guess again.

## Why silence isn't an all-clear

There is **no standardized all-clear signal** in the US. Sirens typically run
about three minutes and stop — while the warning may run another half hour.
Many communities re-sound for new or continuing warnings. The danger ends when
the *warning* expires or is cancelled, which only your information sources
tell you.

## The nighttime gap sirens can't cover

Sirens routinely fail to wake people indoors — they were never meant to. If
your plan for a 2 a.m. tornado is "I'll hear the siren," you don't have a
plan. The indoor equivalent is a **NOAA weather radio with SAME county
alerting** that turns itself on for warnings; our
[emergency radio guide](/best-emergency-radios/) covers which models do this
properly on battery.

## Your three-minute shelter drill

If the confirmation says tornado warning, this is the whole move:

1. **Lowest floor, most interior room** — bathroom, closet, or hallway; put
   as many walls as possible between you and outside. No basement? An
   interior room beats everything else you have.
2. **Grab as you go:** shoes, phone and battery, and head protection —
   a bike helmet is the single cheapest injury-preventer in the house.
   Cover up with a mattress or heavy blankets if you can.
3. **Pets on leashes or in carriers** — a scared dog bolts at exactly the
   wrong moment.
4. **Not shelter:** cars, garages, rooms with wide-span roofs, and windows
   you're tempted to watch from. A **mobile home is never shelter** — know
   your community's nearest sturdy building *before* the season and go at
   the warning, not at the sighting.
5. **Afterward:** stay put for a few minutes (multiple cells travel in
   trains), then check for gas odors before flipping anything on, and treat
   every downed line as [live](/power-outage-near-me/).
