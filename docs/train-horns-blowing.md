# Why Do Train Horns Keep Blowing? (And Crossing Bells That Won't Stop)

**Nearly every train horn you hear is the same federally required signal —
two longs, a short, and a long — sounded for every public crossing, every
train, all night; the exceptions (constant blaring, or bells with no train)
have specific meanings and a specific phone number on a blue sign that
actually fixes them.** Here's how to decode the horns, deal with a stuck
crossing, and — if the sounds have made you curious — hear what the railroad
is saying.

<span class="tldr__label">Key takeaways</span>
**Long-long-short-long** = the required crossing warning, repeated per
crossing — normal, not a message. **Constant/frantic horn** = hazard on the
track; if you can see a person or stuck vehicle: **911 + the blue-sign
number**. **Gates down, no train** = malfunction or hidden stopped train →
call the **blue Emergency Notification sign's 800 number** with the crossing
ID; never drive around gates. **Want them quieter?** That's an FRA **Quiet
Zone** — a city-level project. **Curious what the railroad's saying?**
Rail radio is analog, unencrypted, and easy to hear.

## Decoding what you're hearing

- **Two longs, a short, a long, repeating** — the mandated crossing warning,
  begun ~¼ mile before each crossing. Multiple crossings in town = the
  pattern repeats down the line, which is the whole "why so many horns"
  answer.
- **Continuous or rapid blasts** — the engineer sees something on or near
  the track and cannot stop quickly; a loaded freight needs a mile-plus.
  Visible person or vehicle in the crossing → **911 now**, then the
  blue-sign number.
- **Horns you never used to hear** — a lapsed quiet zone, detoured traffic
  during track work, or new crews running to the letter of the rule.
- **Bells/gates active, no train** — a track-circuit malfunction or a train
  stopped just beyond sight. The **blue Emergency Notification System sign**
  at every crossing carries an 800 number and the crossing's US DOT ID —
  that call reaches the railroad's own dispatcher, who can see the track
  and dispatch a signal maintainer. It's the single most effective
  "civilian" phone number in railroading.

## Blocked crossings and other chronic gripes

A train parked across a crossing for an hour is usually a crew timed out
under hours-of-service law or traffic congestion ahead. Report it with the
crossing ID via the blue sign, and file it on the FRA's public
blocked-crossing portal — those reports are what regulators cite. For
routine horn noise, the fix is a **Quiet Zone**: an FRA process your city
applies for after upgrading crossings; engineers still horn at hazards, but
the every-train pattern stops.

## The sixty-second drill: a car stalled on the tracks

Worth knowing cold, because the instinct (stay and restart) is the fatal one:

1. **Everyone out immediately.** Not after one more crank. A train you can
   see is closer and faster than it looks, and a freight needs a mile-plus
   to stop.
2. **Run toward the oncoming train, angled away from the tracks** (about
   45°). Debris from the impact flies *forward* along the track — standing
   downstream of your own car is the classic fatal error.
3. **Call the blue-sign number** with the crossing ID (it's on the sign),
   then 911. The railroad dispatcher can set signals and radio trains
   faster than anyone else on earth.
4. **Nothing in the car is worth going back for.**

Same logic, smaller stakes: never walk the tracks or use them as a shortcut
or photo backdrop — it's trespassing everywhere, modern welded rail is far
quieter than movies suggest, and a second train on the next track is the
oldest trap in railroading.

## For the newly curious: the railroad is on the radio, in the clear

Railroads run on **analog, unencrypted VHF radio** (the AAR channels around
160–161 MHz) — dispatchers talking to crews, crews calling signals, and the
trackside **defect detectors** that announce each passing train's axle count
and temperature in a synthesized voice. It's some of the easiest, most
listenable traffic in the hobby: any [scanner](/how-police-scanners-work/)
or the [RTL-SDR kit below](#kit) hears it, the
[frequency reference](/scanner-frequencies/) lists the channels, and the
[railfan scanner guide](/best-scanner-for-railfans/) covers the dedicated
options. Between trains, the same receivers answer the rest of this
cluster — [sirens](/sirens-near-me/), [booms](/loud-boom-near-me/), and
[whatever's next](/what-happened-near-me-last-night/).
