## Why analog is all you need at the track

NASCAR Cup, short tracks, and most driver-to-crew and **spotter communications are
analog FM in the UHF race band**. That means a cheap [analog](/scanner-frequencies/)
scanner receives every team as well as any $600
[digital P25](/reference/project-25/) unit — the digital decoding you'd pay for
simply isn't used on the track. Buying a flagship for racing is the most common
first-timer overspend.

## Grabbing a team's frequency with Close Call

Team frequencies are published before some race weekends, but they change and get
reassigned. The reliable method is the BC125AT's **Close Call**: it detects the
strongest nearby transmission and tunes it instantly. Stand near the pits or aim
toward the action and the scanner **locks onto the team keying up right then** — no
frequency list required. Bank the teams you find and scan between them through the
race.

## Gear you actually need at the track

- **A headset is mandatory.** Race cars are painfully loud; a race-day headset
  protects your hearing and lets you hear the scanner over the engines. Plug it
  into the [BC125AT](/reference/uniden-bc125at/)'s 3.5 mm jack.
- **Spare batteries or a battery pack** — a full race day drains a handheld.
- **A short antenna** is fine; you're close to the transmitters.

## Following the whole field with GopherTrunk

A handheld follows one team at a time (though you can bank and scan several). If you
