**[V: title | "Phase Noise" | GT-RF-01.21]**

**[V: metronome — a metronome ticking; zoom into its spectrum: a single thin line that blurs into a fuzzy pedestal of skirts]** [CLIP c1 start]
Phase noise is what keeps a real oscillator from being the perfect metronome it pretends to be. Every radio is built around an oscillator that should produce one pure frequency — a single, infinitely thin spectral line. Phase noise is the rapid, random jitter in that oscillator's phase, and it smears the line into a pedestal of "skirts" spreading out on either side of the carrier. It's the frequency-domain face of timing jitter — and it quietly sets a limit on what a receiver can hear. [CLIP c1 end]

**[V: singer — a singer holds a note; overlay one: fast wobble around the pitch line ("phase noise"); overlay two: the pitch line slowly sagging flat over the evening ("frequency stability")]**
Think of a singer holding a long note. Two different things can go wrong. She can waver — a fast, nervous vibrato around the pitch. Or she can drift — start on pitch and slide slowly flat over the evening. Oscillators fail both ways, and the two failures have different names and different units. The fast waver is phase noise, the short-term jitter. The slow slide is frequency stability, the long-term drift. A source can be excellent at one and mediocre at the other.

**[V: skirts-figure — the article's figure animates: the ideal single spectral line beside the real carrier with skirts; a measuring bracket drops onto the skirt at a 10 kHz offset, reading "−110 dBc/Hz"; then a strong neighbor signal slides in and its energy smears through the skirts into the passband]** [CLIP c2 start]
Mathematically, the oscillator's output is a cosine at its nominal frequency plus a small random phase term that wanders continuously, spreading the carrier's energy into a continuous pedestal on either side. The standard measure is single-sideband phase noise: decibels below the carrier, in each one-hertz slice of bandwidth, at a stated offset. "Minus one hundred ten at ten kilohertz offset" means: ten kilohertz out, each hertz of bandwidth holds one hundred ten decibels less power than the carrier. Phase noise is worst close in and improves as you move out. And here's why receivers care — reciprocal mixing. In a mixer, the local oscillator multiplies with the incoming RF. If the LO carries skirts, a *strong* signal on a nearby channel mixes with those skirts, and its energy lands inside your passband as raised noise — even though it's nominally out of band. A clean LO keeps the skirts low, and the neighbors out. [CLIP c2 end]

**[V: numbers-ladder — cards stack: ×N multiplication → +20·log₁₀(N) dB; ±2 ppm @ 450 MHz → ±900 Hz; plain crystal ±20–30 ppm → TCXO ±0.1–2 ppm → OCXO parts-per-billion → GPSDO]**
A few numbers to calibrate both axes. Multiply an oscillator up to a higher frequency by a factor of N and its phase noise rises by twenty log of N decibels — higher bands are inherently harder. A free-running crystal is good close in; a phase-locked-loop synthesizer blends close-in reference noise against far-out VCO noise through its loop bandwidth. On the stability axis the unit is parts per million: a plus-or-minus two parts-per-million source at four hundred fifty megahertz can wander up to nine hundred hertz. The basic crystals in budget SDR dongles are specified around twenty to thirty parts per million and drift as they warm up. A temperature-compensated crystal, a TCXO, reaches roughly a tenth of a part per million to two; an oven-controlled OCXO reaches parts per billion; and a GPS-disciplined oscillator borrows the atomic clocks in GPS for near-perfect long-term accuracy.

**[V: crowded-band — waterfall with a weak control channel beside a blazing pager transmitter; the noise floor around the weak channel visibly rises]** [CLIP c3 start]
On receive, reciprocal mixing is a real limit in crowded RF environments. A budget tuner with a noisy synthesizer can have its usable sensitivity destroyed near a strong pager or broadcast transmitter — the big neighbor reciprocal-mixes with the LO skirts and buries the weak trunking channel you actually wanted. It's routinely mistaken for poor sensitivity, when the real fault is oscillator spectral purity; a better SDR, or a preselecting band-pass filter that keeps the strong signal out, is the fix. On transmit, the same jitter rotates the constellation from symbol to symbol, inflating error vector magnitude and capping how high an order of modulation the link can carry. [CLIP c3 end]

**[V: gt-tie-in — GopherTrunk chain: SDR front end labeled "phase noise + drift born here"; software boxes "ppm correction" and "carrier-tracking loops" absorbing a slow wander]**
GopherTrunk generates no RF oscillator of its own — whatever phase noise and drift reach its decode chain were contributed by the SDR front end before sampling. What software can fix, it does: a configurable parts-per-million frequency correction handles an oscillator that's stable but simply offset, and carrier-tracking loops pull in residual drift, so a modest, slowly wandering dongle still decodes reliably. What software cannot cure is a source drifting faster than the loops can follow — or LO skirts that have already mixed a neighbor's power into your channel. So a signal that looks strong on the waterfall yet decodes with stubbornly high errors next to a powerful neighbor is a classic reciprocal-mixing signature: it points at the receiver's oscillator, not at the DSP.

**[V: recap | "Phase Noise" | ① Short-term jitter → carrier skirts, in dBc/Hz | ② Skirts + strong neighbor = reciprocal mixing | ③ Stability is the other axis: long-term drift, in ppm]**
So: phase noise is the short-term jitter that grows skirts and lets strong neighbors raise your noise floor, frequency stability is the long-term drift measured in parts per million, and both live in your SDR's oscillator — not in your software. Both write-ups are linked below.
